Clarity, Medication, and a Fire Lit Under Me

Dominicus Johan Nararya • Personal Essay • On Severe ADHD & Execution

If you look closely at my academic record, there is a noticeable gap between my Bachelor’s transcript and what I have produced since. For years, I had no clean explanation for that discrepancy—only the quiet frustration of knowing I could grasp difficult ideas quickly while repeatedly stalling on the ordinary, unglamorous consistency that undergraduate coursework demands.

I was diagnosed with severe ADHD late. Looking back, my Bachelor’s years were spent pushing against an invisible headwind, wondering why maintaining focus, meeting routine deadlines, and keeping conventional momentum felt disproportionately exhausting. Living with that friction—and watching its consequences show up on paper—takes a toll. Without a diagnosis, it is easy to mistake executive dysfunction for a permanent flaw in character.

Clarity, Traction, and Getting Medicated

When the diagnosis finally came, there was hesitation around medication—the usual mix of stigma, pride, and the fear that it might flatten who I am. My experience was the exact opposite. Becoming medicated didn’t turn me into someone else; it simply quieted the static enough for me to access the person I already was.

Medication does not hand you discipline, and it does not do the thinking for you. What it gives you is traction. When ADHD is severe, raw curiosity and ability are not enough—you burn your energy fighting initiation friction before your effort ever has a chance to compound. If you suspect you are dealing with severe, untreated ADHD and keep hitting the same invisible wall despite caring deeply about your work, don’t white-knuckle it out of stubbornness. Get evaluated, seriously consider medication with your clinician, and give yourself a fair shot at reaching your actual potential.

Having a Fire Lit Under Me

The strangest part of finally gaining that clarity wasn’t relief—it was urgency. Once you feel what your baseline is capable of without constant static, you realize how much further you can go. It feels like having a fire lit under me.

I have no interest in treating a late diagnosis as a permanent excuse; if anything, it raised the bar I hold myself to. My focus now is on redeeming myself—holding myself accountable, building real day-to-day discipline, and channeling my curiosity into rigorous, deliberate work that leaves no ambiguity about what I can deliver.

That also means guarding against a subtler trap: ruminating on how things could have gone had I known earlier. It is tempting to replay those undergraduate years when the contrast feels sharp, but dwelling on counterfactuals is just mental wastage—energy stolen from the work in front of you. The only real move is the one Albert Camus described in The Myth of Sisyphus: walk back down the hill, shoulder the boulder, and start again. As long as you start again, the climb is yours—and one must imagine Sisyphus happy.
