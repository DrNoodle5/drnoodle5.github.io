# The Franchise Workforce: Why the Future of Hiring Looks Like Professional Sports

For the better part of a century, the white-collar corporation operated less like a championship team and more like an enormous, slow-moving bureaucracy with a built-in farm system. Every autumn, the ritual repeated itself across university campuses. Consulting firms, investment banks, semiconductor giants, and software companies fanned out to career fairs to recruit vast incoming classes of fresh undergraduates, whom the authors of *The Intelligence Curse* aptly describe as "smart but low-context individuals." Those recruits entered at the wide base of a corporate pyramid. They were handed thankless, repetitive work: formatting slide decks, chasing down software bugs, drafting boilerplate contracts, and verifying spreadsheets. It was inefficient by design, because the work itself was an apprenticeship. Junior employees learned how the machine ran so that, years later, a fraction of them could climb upward to manage the next wave of recruits.

I believe that model is coming to an end. In its place, we are watching the emergence of a hiring paradigm that looks far less like a traditional corporate ladder and far more like the front office of a professional sports franchise.

In high-stakes industries (specifically semiconductors, quantitative finance, and frontier artificial intelligence), companies are already beginning to assemble lean, fiercely scouted rosters built around two distinct roles—rare technical specialists and high-leverage coordinators—rather than sprawling armies of entry-level generalists. Driven by the proliferation of autonomous AI agents and multi-agent swarms, this "franchise model" will not remain confined to Silicon Valley fabs, Wall Street trading desks, and frontier labs. Competitive gravity will pull it across the broader corporate economy, mutating to fit even heavily regulated sectors where human liability remains legally unavoidable. And as the base of the corporate pyramid dissolves into compute, the threshold of verifiable judgment required simply to make the roster will rise sharply—forcing hiring committees to rely on a new ecosystem of scouting combines, from research-intensive graduate apprenticeships to unforgiving public proving grounds.

## Hollowing Out the Pyramid: From Theory to Telemetry

Workplace technology has historically multiplied human workers rather than displacing them, which makes the current shift easy to misread. When companies handed laptops to analysts in the 2000s, output rose, yet the analysts stayed indispensable: a laptop could not accept an open-ended instruction in plain English and return a finished deliverable. Agentic AI breaks that complementarity, a structural reversal laid out in *Pyramid Replacement* (from *The Intelligence Curse*). Once autonomous software can execute junior-level work faster, more consistently, and at a fraction of a human salary—with task-completion horizons already doubling roughly every seven months on METR benchmarks—keeping low-context novices in the execution loop stops looking like a talent investment and starts behaving like operational drag.

Corporations rarely liquidate their junior ranks overnight; cultural inertia, legacy workflows, and early tooling glitches cushion the initial shock. Instead, organizations hollow out from the bottom up in a phased contraction that *The Intelligence Curse* calls **pyramid replacement**. Stage 1 arrives quietly as a slowdown in entry-level recruiting. By Stage 2, campus hiring effectively stalls and junior tiers shrink as senior staff use AI tools to absorb the workload of ten analysts. By Stages 3 and 4, competitive pressure and earnings expectations convert temporary hiring freezes into structural compression across middle management.

This hollowing-out is no longer a theoretical forecast; it is already visible in live organizational telemetry. At roughly five years old, Anthropic was generating two to four times as much revenue per employee as Google and Meta did during their own hyper-growth phases—a benchmark documented in October 2026 by economists Nathan Wilmers, Regina Catipon, Maxim Massenkoff, and Peter McCrory in *How Employment is Changing at Anthropic*.

The engine behind that leverage is the rapid deployment of agent swarms against a compact human headcount. Inside Anthropic, autonomous and interactive AI agent work hours surpassed human work hours for the first time in March 2026. Five months later, interactive agents were logging **six times as many hours as human employees** company-wide, and an astonishing **nineteen times as many hours** inside its research organizations. Engineers saw their code-commit velocity quintuple over six months without any measurable decline in code quality, revert rates, or bug-fix deletions. Meanwhile, non-technical staff began building their own internal software tools, leaping from two coding-agent sessions per month in August 2025 to ninety per month by May 2026.

Predictably, the bottom of the hiring funnel constricted almost immediately. Between early 2025 and 2026, junior employees fell from 21% to 14% of Anthropic's technical headcount, while junior hires plunged from **27% to 9%** of incoming technical talent and dropped to just 6% of open technical roles. By April 2026, roughly a third of machine learning managers and a fifth of software engineering managers reported hiring fewer people because of AI, while a majority had fundamentally rewritten the skills they screened for. At the frontier, the farm system is already closing.

## Why High-Stakes Firms Are Becoming Sports Franchises

If agents are absorbing so much execution, why haven't AI-native companies simply dissolved into tiny collections of solo operators? Anthropic's data reveals something far more nuanced than mass displacement: the remaining humans have become extraordinarily high-leverage bottlenecks. Making sense of why organizations compress into elite, sports-like rosters rather than vanishing altogether requires shifting the unit of analysis from the individual taking a solo benchmark to the firm competing in equilibrium—the core insight of Google DeepMind Institute's October 2026 essay *The Economics of Intelligence* (and its companion working paper, *Organizing Intelligence*) by Alex Imas, Andrew Koh, and Shane Legg.

Human intelligence is rarely uniform; in an empirical experiment across six cognitive domains, the DeepMind researchers showed that human capability is **jagged** in complementary directions—one person excels at mathematical reasoning while struggling with verbal synthesis, and another the reverse. Firms exist to weave those uneven peaks into **mosaic intelligence**, pairing complementary specialists (who outperformed random pairs by 32% in DeepMind's study) alongside skilled coordinators who cut handoff friction.

AI models disrupt this equilibrium on two fronts. First, unlike scarce human talent, a model's competence is infinitely duplicable at the rental cost of compute, eroding the Ricardian scarcity rents of any human skill an agent can reliably match. Second, however, today's models share *overlapping* jaggedness (spinning up a hundred copies of the same checkpoint buys parallel capacity, not complementary cognitive breadth) and remain hampered by brittle multi-agent coordination. Until autonomous systems can pass messy context across long horizons without degrading, they remain constrained by a digital version of Ronald Coase's classic limit on firm size.

Across the four economic regimes DeepMind maps—*Minimal AGI*, *Supercompetent AI*, *Supercooperative AI*, and *Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)*—high-stakes industries are hurtling straight into **Supercompetent AI**. In this regime, agents match or surpass human experts on isolated execution tasks while lagging in autonomous end-to-end coordination. Firms grow dramatically more productive and **radically leaner**, shifting economic rents away from routine execution toward two very different human profiles that are easy to conflate—and fatal for a hiring manager to confuse:

*   **Jagged Specialists (The Star Aces):** Deep domain experts whose rare, non-duplicable technical peaks still sit outside the overlapping capability frontier of current models—the silicon architect who catches a subtle 2-nanometer verification flaw, the quant researcher who spots a non-stationary microstructure edge, or the ML scientist formulating a new pretraining paradigm.
*   **Pure Coordinators (The Point Guards and Head Coaches):** High-leverage generalists and systems orchestrators who may look entirely ordinary on an isolated narrow benchmark, yet dramatically multiply the team's mosaic intelligence by framing ambiguous goals, routing context across mismatched specialists, and keeping agent swarms aligned.

Hiring for those two seats requires opposite scouting criteria. You draft a jagged specialist for depth, idiosyncratic mastery, and first-principles intuition in one unforgiving domain; you draft a pure coordinator for breadth, crisp specification writing, epistemic calibration, and judgment under ambiguity. That structural split—not a vague hunt for generic "superstars"—is what makes the emerging organization look uncannily like a championship sports franchise, whether an NBA roster, a Premier League starting eleven, or a Formula 1 engineering squad:

*   **Economic Roster Caps, Not Rulebook Limits:** Sports leagues keep rosters tiny by artificial fiat—five players on a basketball court, eleven on a pitch. AI-native firms face no league rulebook, yet arrive at a strikingly similar ceiling through economic physics: Coasean coordination drag and human attention limits. Adding fifty low-context junior staff to an agent-saturated chipmaker, hedge fund, or AI lab does not scale output the way adding fifty factory hands once did; it injects communication overhead, review queues, and context handoff errors that actively slow the core squad down. At Anthropic, the ratio of engineers to product managers has stretched to roughly 17 to 1, while clerical and administrative headcount has stayed remarkably flat as agents handle scheduling, ticket triage, and purchase-request reviews.
*   **Human Attention as the Championship Bottleneck:** One Anthropic engineer vividly described daily work as facing "an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention." Internal telemetry backs this up: roughly **80% of interactive agent session time is spent waiting on a human**. Simply reading all the output generated by an engineer's or researcher's AI agents would require an impossible **424 hours per week**, and even in finance and legal departments, it would take 43 and 26 hours per week, respectively.
*   **Meta-Work as the Playbook:** Because humans can no longer manually execute—or even line-by-line read—everything their swarms produce, daily work morphs into what Anthropic calls **"meta-work"**: writing architectural specifications, building visibility dashboards, designing evaluation suites, and deploying review agents to audit worker agents. At Anthropic, meta-work sessions more than doubled from 10% to 22% of all agent usage, while standing instruction sets for agents exploded from a few hundred to 8,500 in a single year. On this roster, jagged specialists set the hard technical invariants and audit frontier breakthroughs, while pure coordinators call the plays and maintain the orchestration harness.

In high-stakes arenas like semiconductor architecture (where a single overlooked verification flaw in a 2-nanometer tape-out can burn tens of millions of dollars), quantitative finance (where microsecond modeling insight separates alpha from ruin), and frontier AI research (where training runs cost hundreds of millions in compute), the margin for error is razor-thin. You do not fill a Formula 1 pit wall or a quant pod with low-context novices hoping they will learn over three years. You construct a balanced, compact roster—scouting globally for both rare jagged specialists and elite coordinators, rewarding them asymmetrically (Anthropic's authors explicitly highlight broad equity ownership as a way for lean teams to share in massive productivity gains), and surrounding every seat with an automated swarm of compute.

## The New Combine: Select Graduate Labs, Rival Arenas, and the Judgment Bar

At first glance, the evidence seems to undercut any case for advanced academic credentials. Frontier models already outperform human experts on PhD-level multiple-choice science benchmarks, as *The Intelligence Curse* notes, and Anthropic's internal interview scorecards show hiring managers placing *greater* weight on **autonomy and judgment** and *less* weight on narrow technical domain expertise and implementation skills.

Worse still, there is a genuine mismatch between how most graduate schools operate and what an AI-native firm actually needs. Traditional academic departments routinely train doctoral students to work slowly and solo on hyper-narrow theoretical questions—the exact opposite of fast-paced commercial meta-work across sprawling agent swarms. Coursework-only Master's degrees are an even weaker proxy: built around structured problem sets and exams, they rarely forge first-principles verification skills at all. And as a matter of simple arithmetic, selective PhD cohorts could never scale to replace an entire economy's vanishing entry-level analyst tier.

Yet dismissing advanced training misses the deeper hiring crisis created by pyramid replacement. Historically, the corporate pyramid *was* the real-world graduate school of business and engineering. A bright twenty-two-year-old with a bachelor's degree could join a chip manufacturer, an investment bank, or a software company knowing very little about production systems; by debugging legacy code, modeling balance sheets by hand, or drafting hundreds of routine memos, they slowly accumulated Polanyi's tacit knowledge—the feel for what "good" looks like and where subtle failure modes hide.

When AI agents swallow that entry-level execution layer, the informal apprenticeship disappears. If a new hire is expected on day one to steer a swarm generating 424 hours of weekly technical output—and to catch subtle architectural hallucinations that pass surface-level unit tests—how can a hiring manager trust an untested undergraduate who has never independently wrestled with an ambiguous, multi-year problem?

In professional sports, when the major-league threshold climbs too high for raw teenagers to survive on the field, leagues depend on specialized collegiate programs and academies to harden and evaluate talent before draft day. In the AI economy, **a bifurcated scouting combine is emerging**—split between *specific* research-intensive graduate environments on one side and unforgiving public arenas on the other.

Not all graduate degrees will survive this filter, and generic diplomas will lose value quickly. The graduate environments that genuinely build "franchise" judgment are **applied, systems-heavy, research-intensive apprenticeships**—engineering and ML labs that ship real empirical systems, thesis-driven industrial Master's programs anchored in messy real-world failure modes, and interdisciplinary PhDs built around adversarial defense rather than passive coursework. Those environments matter not for memorized facts, but because they forge three capabilities that agents cannot reliably self-audit:

1.  **Problem Formulation Over Execution:** Agent swarms excel once handed a crisp objective and an evaluation harness. Researchers in rigorous labs spend years practicing the harder upstream skill: deciding *which* question is worth asking, decomposing an ill-defined frontier problem into tractable hypotheses, and recognizing when an experiment's output is subtly invalid despite looking mathematically plausible.
2.  **First-Principles Verification:** Anthropic's study warns that even when agent-written code shows no higher immediate revert rate than human code, "AI errors may be harder to identify than human ones." Spotting a deep conceptual defect in a silicon layout, a stochastic trading strategy, or a distributed training pipeline demands first-principles mastery forged by breaking and defending real systems.
3.  **A Harder-to-Fake Signal in an AI-Flooded Funnel:** Once AI tools make it trivial for any applicant to generate polished portfolios, take-home exercises, and passable code, standard undergraduate signals lose discriminative power. Faced with thousands of AI-augmented applicants competing for a handful of specialist seats, hiring committees inevitably lean on higher-friction filters: peer-reviewed work, adversarial thesis defenses, and verifiable multi-year research output.

Crucially, however, universities no longer hold a monopoly on the combine—nor can they supply the whole market. Because PhD programs cannot scale across the economy, and because **pure coordinators** often develop orchestration judgment faster in live commercial environments than inside a thesis committee, three rival proving grounds are rapidly competing alongside elite graduate labs:

*   **Open-Source Maintainership:** Leading a widely used repository is already a live dress rehearsal for commercial meta-work—triaging a flood of AI-generated pull requests, guarding architectural invariants, writing specs, and managing contentious human-and-bot collaboration in public.
*   **Startup Founding and Solo-Builder Shipping:** Launching a product with a tiny team (or solo) backed by coding and operations agents forces rapid end-to-end orchestration under real customer and capital constraints—an empirical stress test of autonomy that few classrooms can replicate.
*   **Public Evaluation Benchmarks and Competitive Arenas:** Open leaderboards, empirical research competitions, red-teaming bounties, and public technical artifacts allow scouts to audit a candidate's problem formulation, verification rigor, and taste directly, without relying on institutional pedigree.

This split mirrors the two franchise seats from Section 2. Rigorous research-oriented Master's and PhD labs will increasingly act as the baseline feeder for **jagged technical specialists** in deep scientific and engineering arenas, while open-source maintainership, founder trenches, and public evaluation arenas will rival—and often beat—universities as the primary combine for **pure coordinators** and applied meta-work directors. Even roles that were once purely operational are moving up this curve: at Anthropic, a product content team that automated its own UI copywriting using context graphs pivoted into writing evaluation benchmarks and behavioral prompts to steer Claude itself, while an executive assistant who automated scheduling and inbox triage evolved into a strategic "chief of staff." Across every function, the floor is rising—and universities that refuse to re-engineer graduate training around high-autonomy orchestration and real-world verification will watch front offices draft straight past campus.

## Diffusion Beyond the Frontier: When Moneyball Meets the Liability Bottleneck

A fair skeptic will point out that Anthropic, Nvidia, Jane Street, and DeepMind are rarefied outliers—the New York Yankees and Manchester City of the global economy—and question whether their roster logic can ever travel to healthcare networks, law firms, insurance carriers, or auditing practices.

The strongest argument against rapid diffusion is not merely cultural inertia or cautious executives. In regulated professions, human headcount is anchored by **legal liability and fiduciary accountability**. A hospital system, corporate law firm, or property insurer does not employ licensed physicians, attorneys, CPAs, and actuaries simply out of habit; statutes and courts require a credentialed human to bear malpractice, regulatory, and fiduciary risk when something goes wrong. An agent swarm cannot hold a medical license, be disbarred, testify under oath, or absorb civil liability when it hallucinates a nonexistent precedent or misses a rare clinical contraindication.

Yet the liability bottleneck does not preserve the traditional corporate pyramid—it **reshapes the franchise model into a new equilibrium**. Competitive gravity still works relentlessly on the execution layer underneath. Just as analytics-driven roster construction (*Moneyball*) spread from a few scrappy baseball front offices across every league once legacy scouting proved uncompetitive, lean AI-native entrants can undercut traditional professional-service incumbents on turnaround speed and cost—putting intense margin pressure on legacy firms whenever an economic downturn or earnings miss arrives, the exact competitive dynamic outlined in *Pyramid Replacement*.

What emerges inside regulated industries is not an autonomous, human-free enterprise, but a lean roster of **high-liability "sign-off captains"** overseeing fleets of specialized agents. A corporate law practice no longer needs thirty first-year associates manually reviewing discovery documents and redlining boilerplate NDAs if agents handle first-pass drafting; instead, legal accountability concentrates on a smaller core of seasoned partners and senior counsel whose job is auditing edge cases, verifying citations, negotiating judgment calls, and placing their bar license behind the final signature. The same pattern applies to attending physicians reviewing AI-synthesized diagnostics, chief underwriters supervising automated risk scoring, and lead auditors inspecting continuous AI-driven financial checks.

Early signals of how fast these support and compliance workflows compress are already visible outside engineering. Inside Anthropic's own non-technical departments, legal staff roughly tripled their contract-processing throughput; sales representatives automated away 20% of routine CRM logging and began building custom operational dashboards in three to four hours using coding agents; finance managers deployed autonomous review agents to audit spending. None of those workflows are unique to a frontier AI lab—every Fortune 500 enterprise runs legal, finance, compliance, and operations teams.

And anyone waiting for dramatic headline layoffs in legacy sectors before believing the shift is underway is watching a lagging indicator. As DeepMind's *Minimal AGI* analysis demonstrates, **wage effects arrive before visible organizational restructuring**: even while hospitals, banks, and law firms preserve legacy headcount on paper, the option to rent machine execution at the price of compute begins compressing salaries for routine matched roles while bidding up premiums for licensed captains, rare specialists, and skilled orchestrators. Ironically, the liability bottleneck makes the vanishing apprenticeship crisis *even sharper* in regulated fields—because society cannot hand malpractice or fiduciary sign-off authority to a junior who never learned the craft through hands-on fundamentals, forcing medical residencies, legal clinics, and actuarial tracks to reinvent how judgment is proven before anyone earns the captain's armband.

## Playing in a League Without a Ceiling

No honest view of this transition can ignore the harder question looming beyond the horizon of the franchise workforce: is this lean human-orchestrated equilibrium permanent, or merely a high-stakes transitional chapter of the late 2020s and 2030s?

Neither the empirical trajectory nor formal organizational economics promises a permanent safe harbor. In the later stages of pyramid replacement sketched in *The Intelligence Curse* (Stages 5 and 6), once AI systems retain coherent institutional memory and execute long-horizon strategy, compression pushes past junior analysts into middle management and even the C-suite. DeepMind's framework converges on the same inflection point from formal firm theory: if AI research cracks reliable multi-agent coordination—crossing the **Supercooperative AI** threshold and shattering the Coasean ceiling—then organizations composed purely of seamlessly coordinating agents could abruptly outscale and outcompete human-led firms across standardized markets like software, logistics, and finance. Under full Artificial Superintelligence, even specialized niches erode, leaving human comparative advantage concentrated in relational domains—care, mentorship, governance, and live craft—where human presence and accountability are the product itself.

Yet between today and that distant theoretical endpoint lies a decisive, potentially decades-long era governed by Supercompetent AI, human-attention bottlenecks, and irreducible legal accountability. Throughout this era, machines will generate ocean-sized volumes of execution, while humans remain the scarce arbiters of direction, verification, liability, and taste.

In that world, the old social contract of career building has already been rewritten. The generous, forgiving corporate pyramid—where you could show up with a bachelor's degree, learn the trade through years of supervised grunt work, and steadily climb the ranks—is giving way to a fiercely selective professional league. Chips, finance, and frontier AI are simply the first divisions to unveil the new roster rules, while regulated industries are already adapting them around licensed captains. For the next generation of workers, universities, and institutions, the imperative is clear: when agent swarms handle the fundamentals at the cost of electricity, making the roster demands more than baseline compliance. Whether forged in a rigorous research lab, an open-source repository, or a founder's trenches, entry now requires the verifiable judgment, specialized mastery, or orchestration leverage of someone ready to play on day one.
