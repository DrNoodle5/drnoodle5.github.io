# The Franchise Workforce: Why the Future of Hiring Looks Like Professional Sports

For the better part of a century, the white-collar corporation operated less like a championship team and more like an enormous, slow-moving bureaucracy with a built-in farm system. Every autumn, the ritual repeated itself across university campuses. Consulting firms, investment banks, semiconductor giants, and software companies fanned out to career fairs to recruit vast incoming classes of fresh undergraduates, whom the authors of *The Intelligence Curse* aptly describe as "smart but low-context individuals." Those recruits entered at the wide base of a corporate pyramid. They were handed thankless, repetitive work: formatting slide decks, chasing down software bugs, drafting boilerplate contracts, and verifying spreadsheets. It was inefficient by design, because the work itself was an apprenticeship. Junior employees learned how the machine ran so that, years later, a fraction of them could climb upward to manage the next wave of recruits.

I believe that model is coming to an end. In its place, we are watching the emergence of a hiring paradigm that looks far less like a traditional corporate ladder and far more like the front office of a professional sports franchise. 

In high-stakes industries (specifically semiconductors, quantitative finance, and frontier artificial intelligence), companies are already beginning to assemble lean, fiercely scouted rosters of elite human specialists and strategic orchestrators, rather than sprawling armies of entry-level generalists. Driven by the proliferation of autonomous AI agents and multi-agent swarms, this "franchise model" will not remain confined to Silicon Valley fabs, Wall Street trading desks, and frontier labs. Competitive gravity will pull it across the broader corporate economy. And as the base of the corporate pyramid dissolves into compute, the consequences for human talent will be unmistakable: the threshold of competence required simply to make the roster will rise sharply, turning advanced degrees (Master's programs, PhDs, and deep research apprenticeships) from optional prestige markers into the new baseline of entry.

## Hollowing Out the Pyramid: From Theory to Telemetry

To understand why hiring is mutating into a professional draft, we first have to look at why artificial intelligence breaks the historical pattern of workplace technology. In the essay *Pyramid Replacement* from *The Intelligence Curse*, the authors draw a sharp contrast between the laptops of the 2000s and the agentic systems of the late 2020s. When companies bought laptops for their analysts, productivity rose, but the analysts remained indispensable; a laptop could not take an open-ended instruction in plain English and return a finished deliverable. Modern AI agents reverse that dependency. When an autonomous system can execute junior-level work faster, more consistently, and at a fraction of the cost of a human salary (with task-completion horizons doubling roughly every seven months, according to METR benchmarks cited in the essay), keeping low-context humans in the loop stops being an investment and starts becoming a drag.

*The Intelligence Curse* maps this transformation as **pyramid replacement**, a phased hollowing out of white-collar organizations from the bottom up. Firms rarely fire their entire junior class overnight; inertia, culture, and early technical glitches hold them back. Instead, Stage 1 begins quietly with a slowdown in entry-level hiring. By Stage 2, entry-level recruiting effectively stops and junior tiers shrink as senior employees use AI tools to absorb the workload of ten analysts. By Stages 3 and 4, competitive pressure and earnings expectations turn hiring freezes into structural compression across middle management.

If *Pyramid Replacement* reads like a stark theoretical forecast, Anthropic's October 2026 empirical study, *How Employment is Changing at Anthropic*, reads like live telemetry from the future arriving right on schedule. Authored by economists Nathan Wilmers, Regina Catipon, Maxim Massenkoff, and Peter McCrory, the study examines Anthropic's own workforce as a leading indicator of an AI-native organization. At roughly five years old, Anthropic was already generating two to four times as much revenue per employee as Google and Meta did during their own hyper-growth phases.

The mechanism behind that leverage is the rapid deployment of agent swarms. According to Anthropic's internal telemetry, autonomous and interactive AI agent work hours surpassed human work hours for the first time in March 2026. By August 2026, interactive AI agents logged **six times as many hours as human employees** across the company, and an astonishing **nineteen times as many hours** inside its research organizations. Engineers saw their code-commit velocity quintuple over six months without any measurable decline in code quality, revert rates, or bug-fix deletions. Non-technical staff began building their own software tools, leaping from two coding-agent sessions per month in August 2025 to ninety per month by May 2026.

And precisely as *Pyramid Replacement* predicted, the bottom of the hiring funnel began to narrow almost immediately. Between early 2025 and 2026, junior employees fell from 21% to 14% of Anthropic's technical headcount. Even more tellingly, junior technical hires plunged from **27% to 9%** of incoming technical talent, dropping to just 6% of open technical roles. By April 2026, roughly a third of machine learning managers and a fifth of software engineering managers reported hiring fewer people because of AI, while a majority reported fundamentally changing the skills they looked for. The farm system was already shrinking at the frontier.

## Why High-Stakes Firms Are Becoming Sports Franchises

Yet Anthropic's data also reveals something far more nuanced than simple mass replacement: the remaining humans at the company are not obsolete; they have become extraordinarily high-leverage bottlenecks. To explain why high-stakes organizations are compressing into elite, sports-like rosters rather than vanishing altogether, we need the economic framework introduced in Google DeepMind Institute's October 2026 essay, *The Economics of Intelligence* (alongside its companion working paper, *Organizing Intelligence*), by Alex Imas, Andrew Koh, and Shane Legg.

Imas, Koh, and Legg argue that most AI discourse focuses on the wrong economic unit: the individual agent taking a solo benchmark. Economies are not built out of isolated individuals; they are built out of organizations competing in equilibrium. In an empirical experiment across six cognitive domains, the DeepMind researchers demonstrated that human intelligence is **jagged** in complementary directions: one person excels at math while struggling with verbal synthesis, and another the reverse. Firms exist to weave these uneven profiles into **mosaic intelligence**, pairing complementary specialists (who outperformed random pairs by 32% in DeepMind's study) alongside skilled coordinators who reduce handoff friction.

AI models disrupt this equilibrium in two ways. First, unlike scarce human talent, an AI model's competence is infinitely duplicable at the rental cost of compute, immediately eroding the Ricardian scarcity rents of any human skill a model can match. Second, however, AI models currently share *overlapping* jaggedness (spinning up a hundred copies of the same model adds capacity, not complementary breadth), and they remain constrained by poor multi-agent coordination. Until AI agents learn to coordinate seamlessly without losing context, they remain bound by a version of Ronald Coase's classic limit on firm size.

DeepMind maps this landscape into four economic regimes: *Minimal AGI*, *Supercompetent AI*, *Supercooperative AI*, and *Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)*. Right now, high-stakes industries are accelerating headlong into the **Supercompetent AI** regime. In this regime, AI models match or exceed human experts at isolated execution tasks, but still lag in autonomous end-to-end coordination. DeepMind's model predicts a striking structural result: firms become dramatically more productive and **radically leaner**, without necessarily growing larger in headcount. The economic premium shifts aggressively away from routine execution toward human **judgment, orchestration, and rare, non-duplicable specialization**.

Look at what that economic structure actually resembles in the real world. It is the exact architecture of a championship sports franchise—an NBA roster, a Premier League starting eleven, or a Formula 1 engineering squad:

*   **Strict Roster Caps and Extreme Leverage:** In basketball, you cannot beat a five-player superteam by putting fifty mediocre players on the court. Similarly, in an AI-native chipmaker, hedge fund, or AI lab, adding average junior staff creates coordination drag rather than output. Anthropic's own internal ratio of engineers to product managers has stretched to roughly 17 to 1, while clerical and administrative headcount has stayed remarkably flat as agents handle scheduling, ticket triage, and purchase-request reviews.
*   **Human Attention as the Championship Bottleneck:** One Anthropic engineer vividly described daily work as facing "an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention." Internal telemetry backs this up: roughly **80% of interactive agent session time is spent waiting on a human**. Simply reading all the output generated by an engineer's or researcher's AI agents would require an impossible **424 hours per week**, and even in finance and legal departments, it would take 43 and 26 hours per week, respectively.
*   **The Rise of the Quarterback and the Coach:** Because humans can no longer physically execute or even manually read every line of output, their job morphs into what Anthropic calls **"meta-work"**: writing architectural specifications, building visibility dashboards, designing evaluation suites, and deploying review agents to audit worker agents. At Anthropic, meta-work sessions more than doubled from 10% to 22% of all agent usage, while standing instruction sets for agents exploded from a few hundred to 8,500 in a single year.

In high-stakes arenas like semiconductor architecture (where a single overlooked verification flaw in a 2-nanometer tape-out can burn tens of millions of dollars), quantitative finance (where microsecond modeling insight separates alpha from ruin), and frontier AI research (where training runs cost hundreds of millions in compute), the margin for error is razor-thin. You do not fill a Formula 1 pit wall or a quant pod with low-context novices hoping they will learn over three years. You scout globally for generational talent, pay them asymmetrically (Anthropic's authors explicitly highlight broad equity ownership as a way for lean teams to share in massive productivity gains), and surround each franchise player with an automated swarm of compute.

## The New Combine: Why Master's and PhDs Become the Baseline

At first glance, Anthropic's hiring data appears to contain a contradiction for my thesis. When analyzing internal interview scorecards, the researchers found that hiring managers were placing *greater* weight on **autonomy and judgment** and *less* weight on narrow technical domain expertise and implementation skills. A casual observer might read that and conclude that formal credentials (Master's degrees and PhDs) are about to matter less in the age of AI.

I believe the exact opposite is true. When execution is cheap, **the bar for verifiable judgment skyrockets**—and advanced degrees are poised to become the primary proving ground for that judgment.

Consider the structural crisis created by *Pyramid Replacement*. Historically, the corporate pyramid *was* the graduate school of the business world. A bright twenty-two-year-old with a bachelor's degree could join a chip manufacturer, an investment bank, or a tech firm knowing very little about real-world systems. By debugging legacy code, modeling balance sheets by hand, or drafting hundreds of routine memos, they slowly accumulated Polanyi's tacit knowledge, the intuition for what "good" looks like and where hidden failure modes lurk.

When AI agents absorb that entire entry-level execution layer, that informal apprenticeship vanishes. If an organization expects a new hire on day one to direct a swarm of agents generating 424 hours of weekly technical output, and to catch subtle architectural hallucinations that pass surface-level unit tests, how can a hiring manager trust a twenty-two-year-old generalist who has never independently wrestled with an ambiguous, multi-year problem?

In professional sports, when the major-league threshold gets too high for raw teenagers to survive on the field, leagues rely heavily on elite collegiate programs and academies to filter, harden, and evaluate talent before draft day. In the knowledge economy, **Master's and PhD programs will become the new scouting combine.**

A rigorous PhD or research-intensive Master's degree is rarely valuable solely because of the narrow facts a student memorizes; after all, frontier AI models already outperform human experts on PhD-level multiple-choice science benchmarks, as *The Intelligence Curse* notes. Rather, graduate training is valuable because it is one of the few remaining institutions explicitly designed to forge **autonomy, taste, and epistemic judgment under uncertainty**:

1.  **Problem Formulation Over Execution:** AI swarms excel when given a crisp objective and a clear evaluation harness. Graduate researchers spend years learning the hardest meta-skill of all: deciding *which* question is worth asking, how to decompose an ill-defined frontier problem into tractable hypotheses, and when an experiment's results are subtly flawed despite looking mathematically plausible.
2.  **First-Principles Verification:** Anthropic's study notes that while agent-written code shows no higher immediate revert rate than human code, "AI errors may be harder to identify than human ones." Spotting a deep conceptual error in a silicon layout, a stochastic trading strategy, or a distributed training pipeline requires mastery of first principles, the exact depth cultivated through graduate-level rigor.
3.  **A Credible Signal in a Flooded Market:** As AI tools make it trivial for anyone to produce polished portfolios, take-home assignments, and passable code, traditional undergraduate signals lose their discriminative power. Faced with thousands of AI-augmented applicants for a tiny handful of franchise roster spots, high-stakes hiring committees will inevitably lean on deeper, harder-to-fake filters: peer-reviewed research, complex thesis defenses, systems-level Master's work, and PhD apprenticeships.

Even roles that were once purely operational are shifting upward in conceptual complexity. In Anthropic's study, a product content team that automated its own UI copywriting using context graphs did not disappear; it pivoted into writing evaluation benchmarks and behavioral prompts to steer Claude itself. An executive assistant who automated scheduling and inbox triage evolved into a strategic "chief of staff." Across every function, the floor is rising. The work that remains for humans demands the analytical maturity of a seasoned specialist, compressing years of former on-the-job growth into pre-employment preparation.

## Diffusion Beyond the Frontier: When Every Company Plays Moneyball

Skeptics might reasonably ask whether Anthropic, Nvidia, Jane Street, and DeepMind are simply rarefied outliers (the New York Yankees and Manchester City of the global economy) whose talent strategies will never apply to insurance firms, healthcare networks, law practices, or logistics providers.

Both economic theory and early empirical evidence suggest otherwise. In sports, analytics-driven roster construction (*Moneyball*) began with a few innovative front offices and quickly became mandatory across every league, because any franchise that clung to legacy scouting was systematically outcompeted. The same unforgiving competitive logic drives *Pyramid Replacement*. As *The Intelligence Curse* emphasizes, diffusion barriers such as regulation, cultural inertia, and leadership skepticism can slow adoption in traditional industries, but they cannot halt it indefinitely. The moment a lean, AI-native competitor enters a legacy market (delivering legal contract analysis, financial auditing, or enterprise software with a tenth of the headcount and a fraction of the overhead), incumbents face an existential choice during the very next economic downturn or earnings miss: flatten the pyramid, or lose the market.

DeepMind's *Economics of Intelligence* reinforces this timeline with a sobering caveat for those who point to currently stable aggregate employment numbers as proof that nothing is changing. Under DeepMind's *Minimal AGI* model, **wage effects arrive before visible organizational restructuring**. Even while traditional firms keep their legacy headcounts intact, the ability to rent machine competence at the price of compute begins compressing salaries for matched, routine roles while inflating premiums for top coordinators and rare jagged specialists. By the time layoffs or hiring freezes hit the headlines in mid-market industries, the underlying economics have already shifted.

We can already see the blueprints for broader diffusion inside Anthropic's non-technical departments. Legal staff roughly tripled their contract processing throughput; sales representatives automated away 20% of their routine CRM logging and began building custom operational dashboards in three to four hours using coding agents; finance managers spun up autonomous review agents to audit spending. None of these workflows are unique to an AI lab. Every Fortune 500 enterprise has legal teams, sales floors, finance departments, and operations managers. As agentic scaffolds become turnkey enterprise software, the franchise workforce, tiny cores of deeply trained, credentialed human directors orchestrating vast pools of digital labor, will migrate from the frontier of chips, finance, and AI into the mainstream economy.

## Playing in a League Without a Ceiling

No honest assessment of these three works can ignore the shadow looming just beyond the horizon of the franchise workforce. If my thesis describes the emerging equilibrium of the late 2020s and 2030s, both *The Intelligence Curse* and DeepMind's *Economics of Intelligence* force us to ask whether that equilibrium is permanent, or merely a high-stakes transitional chapter.

In *The Intelligence Curse*, pyramid replacement does not politely stop at Stage 3 to protect PhDs and senior orchestrators. In Stages 5 and 6, as models gain the ability to hold complete institutional memory and execute long-horizon strategy, even middle management and the C-suite face displacement. DeepMind arrives at a remarkably similar inflection point through formal organizational economics: if AI research eventually cracks multi-agent coordination, crossing the **Supercooperative AI** threshold and shattering the Coasean ceiling, then firms composed purely of seamlessly coordinating AI agents could abruptly outscale and outcompete human-led organizations in deep, standardized markets like software, logistics, and finance. Under full Artificial Superintelligence, even niche shelters erode, leaving human comparative advantage concentrated in relational domains (care, mentorship, governance, live craft) where human presence itself is the product.

Yet between today and that distant theoretical endpoint lies a decisive, potentially decades-long era defined by DeepMind's *Supercompetent AI* regime and Anthropic's human-attention bottleneck. Throughout this era, machines will generate ocean-sized volumes of execution, while humans remain the scarce arbiters of direction, accountability, ethics, and taste.

In that world, the social contract of career building has already been rewritten. The generous, forgiving corporate pyramid, where you could show up with a bachelor's degree, learn the trade by doing the grunt work, and slowly climb the ranks, is giving way to a fiercely selective professional league. Chips, finance, and AI are simply the first divisions to unveil the new roster rules, but every industry is studying the tape. For the next generation of workers, educators, and institutions, the imperative is clear: when agent swarms handle the fundamentals at the cost of electricity, making the team will require more than baseline competence. It will demand the deep mastery, independent judgment, and advanced rigor of a franchise player.
